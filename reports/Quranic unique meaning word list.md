# Quranic Vocabulary Fits 2048 Meanings, Not 8192

A word list in which every entry has its own meaning is possible. Each entry must be a **lemma**: the dictionary headword that groups all the grammatical forms of one word, so ربكم, ربهم and ربه ("your/their/his Lord") become the single entry رَبّ. The list must also have **2048 entries**. Depending on the counting scheme, the whole Quran contains about **3,400 to 4,800 lemmas**. After removing particles, proper names, sacred terms and words that translate the same way, an estimated **2,300 to 2,900 distinct meanings** remain. That is enough for 2048, too few for 4096, and far too few for 8192. A verbs-only list fails at every size: the Quran has only about **1,475 verb lemmas**. About 1,415 of them stay different when typed without diacritics, and only about 724 stay if no two words may be one typo apart. The current Bucks list shows why the word-form approach has to go. Measured directly, **3,840 of its 8,192 entries (47%) clash with another entry when typed without diacritics**, and 61 entries are spellings of the owner's own example, ربكم/ربهم/ربه. Moving to 2048 keeps the wallet as secure as today. Bucks derives the seed from the entropy, so phrases of **13 words (128-bit) and 25 words (256-bit)** keep the checksum at least as strong as today's 11-word and 21-word phrases. No open data set offers per-lemma meanings in several languages under terms that suit closed-source commercial software. So Bucks needs three things: written permission for its Arabic lemma data, spellings taken from Tanzil with credit, and its own commissioned translations. No fatwa covers seed phrases. However, rulings on the mushaf, on coins and on the divine names support four choices: write ordinary dictionary words in modern spelling, leave out names of God, prophets and worship terms, avoid the label "Quranic words", and include the list in any Shariah review.

## The whole Quran contains fewer than 4,800 dictionary words

The question "how many unique words does the Quran have?" has several correct answers, because each level of counting groups words differently. A **token** is one written word in the running text. A **word form** is one distinct spelling, so ربكم and ربهم are two forms. A **lemma** is the dictionary headword shared by forms that differ only by grammar: person, number, tense, case or an attached pronoun. A **root** is the family of three or four consonants, such as ع ل م, from which many lemmas with different meanings are built. The Quranic Arabic Corpus (QAC) defines a lemma as a group of word forms that "do not vary in meaning", which is exactly the owner's goal ([QAC lemmas](https://corpus.quran.com/lemmas.jsp)). Each step down this ladder divides the count by roughly three to five.

| Level | What one entry groups | Count | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tokens | every written word, repeats included | 77,429–77,430 | [QuranMorph](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18148v1); [Wikipedia: QAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quranic_Arabic_Corpus) |
| Word forms with diacritics | each spelling, prefixes and pronouns attached | 18,993–19,009 | [Dukes, quoted on blog](http://quickestwaytoquran.blogspot.com/2013/08/number-of-unique-words-in-quran.html); [QuranMorph](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18148v1) |
| Word forms without diacritics | same, diacritics and letter variants removed | 14,543–14,972 (older count: 14,870) | computed from [QAC data](https://github.com/mustafa0x/quran-morphology); [Harrag et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.03232) |
| Stems | forms without prefixes and suffixes | 12,183 | [Dukes, quoted on blog](http://quickestwaytoquran.blogspot.com/2013/08/number-of-unique-words-in-quran.html) |
| Lemmas, all parts of speech | one dictionary headword | 3,382 / 3,680 / 4,616 / 4,776 | [Dukes](http://quickestwaytoquran.blogspot.com/2013/08/number-of-unique-words-in-quran.html); [QAC website](https://corpus.quran.com/lemmas.jsp); [QuranMorph](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18148v1); [QAC data](https://github.com/mustafa0x/quran-morphology) |
| Verb lemmas | one root plus one verb pattern (Form I–X) | 1,473 / 1,475 / 1,479 | [QAC data](https://github.com/mustafa0x/quran-morphology); [QAC verbs](https://corpus.quran.com/verbs.jsp); [QuranMorph](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18148v1) |
| Roots | consonant family | 1,641 / 1,651 / 1,685 | [quran.so](https://quran.so/roots.md); [ParallelQuran](https://parallelquran.com/language-tools/); [Dukes](http://quickestwaytoquran.blogspot.com/2013/08/number-of-unique-words-in-quran.html) |

### Different totals come from different counting rules, not from errors

The token counts differ because the two scripts put spaces in different places. The Uthmani script joins the vocative يا to the next word (يَٰٓأَيُّهَا), while ordinary script writes يا أيها as two words. Popular counts such as 77,797 give no method ([Harrag et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.03232)). Word-form counts differ because attached prefixes (و ف ب ل ال) and pronoun endings make ربكم, ربهم and ربه separate forms, and because diacritics split كَتَبَ from كُتِبَ. Removing diacritics brings about 19,000 forms down to about 15,000. Folding alef, ya and ta marbuta takes a little more off. When the research team folded the QAC data step by step, the count fell from 18,993 to 14,972 and then to 14,543 ([QAC data](https://github.com/mustafa0x/quran-morphology)).

The lemma counts show the widest spread, and they matter most for Bucks. The QAC website lists **3,680** lemmas. It lists some lemmas twice when they act as two parts of speech; for example, وَهَّاب appears as both a noun and an adjective ([QAC lemmas p. 41](https://corpus.quran.com/lemmas.jsp?page=41)). Dukes's **3,382** is an older figure, and the 2011 release notes describe changes to the lemma scheme ([QAC release notes](https://corpus.quran.com/releasenotes.jsp)). QuranMorph links each word to the Qabas lexicon, which splits lemmas more finely. It reports **4,616** lemmas: 3,057 nouns, 1,479 verbs and 173 function words. Those three parts add up to 4,709, so about 93 lemmas seem to be counted in two classes ([QuranMorph](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18148v1)). The research team counted **4,776** distinct lemma strings in a corrected copy of the QAC data file, and **4,270** after removing diacritics and folding letter variants ([mustafa0x/quran-morphology](https://github.com/mustafa0x/quran-morphology)). Why this file count is higher than the website's 3,680 was not established. Spelling variants of one lemma and the fork's corrections are the likely causes. Root counts (1,641–1,685) differ in how they treat weak and hamza roots, foreign names and four-letter roots. The verb counts nearly agree (1,473–1,479), and these verbs come from **940 roots** ([QAC data](https://github.com/mustafa0x/quran-morphology)). For Bucks the exact figure matters less than the ceiling. Every scheme lands below 4,800 lemmas, which is less than 60% of 8,192, before a single word is removed as unsafe, sacred or a repeated meaning.

### The current 8192 list is a word-form list, and the damage is measurable

The current file `wordlists/bip_8192.txt` was measured during this review. **5,278 of its 8,192 entries carry Quranic pause or recitation marks** (Unicode U+06D6–U+06ED). Removing only those marks leaves 7,258 distinct entries. So **934 entries differ from another entry only by a pause sign**, for example ءَابَاۤءَكُمۡ and ءَابَاۤءَكُمۡۖ. Removing all diacritics leaves 5,791 distinct strings. Folding the alef, hamza, ya and ta marbuta variants as well leaves 5,651. The owner's figure of 5,731 sits between these, depending on the exact rules used. Under full folding, **1,299 groups containing 3,840 entries** clash. The largest group has **21 entries that all type as ربكم**. Sixty-one entries are spellings of ربكم, ربهم or ربه alone, about 120 are forms of رب in total, at least 15 are forms of الله or إله, 334 begin with the article ال (alone or after و ف ب ك ل), and 127 different spellings are two letters or shorter. In practice, a user who types ربكم on a normal keyboard matches 21 different index numbers, and the wallet cannot tell which one was meant. The 2026 multilingual BIP draft says a wallet in that situation must reject the word and ask the user, and must never pick one silently ([BIP PR #2200](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/pull/2200)). The current list needs to be replaced for typing safety alone, apart from the meaning question.

## Only lemmas reach 2048; roots, verbs and word forms fail

The four selection levels lead to clearly different results. The table combines the published counts with the research team's estimates. Estimates are marked with ≈.

| Level | Raw count | Distinct meanings after cleaning | 2048 | 4096 | 8192 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Roots | 1,641–1,685 | ≈1,500–1,600, and each root is a family of meanings, not one meaning | No | No | No |
| Lemmas, all parts of speech | 3,382–4,776 (4,270 without diacritics) | ≈3,000–3,300 content lemmas, then ≈2,300–2,900 distinct English meanings | Yes, but tight | No | No |
| Verb lemmas only | 1,473–1,479 (1,415 without diacritics) | ≈1,300–1,450 meanings; 724 under a strict one-typo rule | No | No | No |
| Word forms | ≈19,000 (≈14,500–15,000 without diacritics) | Meanings repeat ("he said", "they said", "we said") | Numbers yes, meanings no | Numbers yes, meanings no | Only by keeping diacritic and suffix variants |

Roots fail because even the highest count (1,685) is 363 short of 2048. A root also has no single meaning. The root ع ل م produces "to know" (عَلِمَ), "to teach" (عَلَّمَ), "to learn" (تَعَلَّمَ), "knowledge", "worlds" and "flags" ([QAC Quran Dictionary, root ʿlm](https://corpus.quran.com/qurandictionary.jsp?q=Elm)). Word forms reach big numbers only by counting grammar as meaning, which is the duplication the owner wants to remove. That leaves lemmas. Start from the QAC website's 3,680 and subtract the function words (QuranMorph counts 173 ([QuranMorph](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18148v1))), the proper names, the divine names, the lemmas listed under two parts of speech and the very short words. About 3,000–3,300 content lemmas remain. Many of these still share one translation, since the Quran has several words for fire and Hell, for punishment, for "come", "see" and "give". Merging them gives roughly 2,300–2,900 distinct English meanings. **These last figures are estimates.** No published source counts distinct glosses per lemma, so the first build step must measure the real number. Starting from Qabas's finer 4,616 lemmas raises the raw ceiling, but merging synonyms still leaves the result very likely below 4096 ([QuranMorph](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18148v1)).

The Quran's vocabulary also has a long tail of rare words, and a 2048 list cannot avoid it. In the QAC frequency list, entries 2001–2026 occur twice, and **the first word that occurs only once is entry 2,027** ([QAC lemmas p. 41](https://corpus.quran.com/lemmas.jsp?page=41)). So **1,654 of 3,680 lemma entries (45%) occur once**, and only 2,026 occur twice or more, a count that still includes particles and names. A 2048-entry list must therefore include several hundred once-only words. If all once-only words were banned, only about 1,600–1,800 entries would remain, and 2048 would be out of reach. Classical sources do not always agree on how to analyse a word: Elie Wardini, who compiled a complete Quran word list, notes that dictionaries and commentaries sometimes disagree on a word's root and lemma ([Gorgias Press](https://gorgiaspress.com/the-quran-word-list-tome-1)). Any word whose meaning is disputed should be dropped, because each entry needs one fixed translation in every language. One listed example of a Quranic once-only word, ṣamad ([JQST](https://jqst.ut.ac.ir/article_54280_en.html)), is also the exclusive divine name al-Ṣamad, so it would be excluded for religious reasons anyway.

### A strict one-typo rule decides whether 2048 survives

The research notes disagree on whether 2048 is achievable, and the reason is that they measure different rules. The vocabulary-statistics work counted **meanings only** and found 2048 feasible, with a margin of a few hundred. The list-design work also measured **typo distance**: it checked whether any two entries are only one edit apart, meaning one letter changed, added or removed, or two neighbouring letters swapped. Under the strictest published rule, SLIP-39's "no two words within one edit" ([SLIP-0039](https://github.com/satoshilabs/slips/blob/master/slip-0039.md)), a greedy selection kept only **1,818 of the 4,270 folded Quranic lemmas** ([QAC data](https://github.com/mustafa0x/quran-morphology)). That pool still included names, particles and sacred words. A greedy pass gives a minimum, and a better optimiser might find more. Removing the excluded categories pushes the number down again. The same design note still called 2048 "feasible", but only if the one-edit ban applies to short words rather than to every pair. The two positions can be reconciled like this. **With uniqueness after folding plus a one-edit ban on words of four letters or fewer, 2048 is probably reachable. With a one-edit ban on every pair plus the religious exclusions, 2048 has not been shown to be reachable and probably is not.** The size that safely fits the strictest rule is 1024.

Arabic makes the strict rule expensive because most words are built on three-letter roots. Nearly four in five Quranic verbs (**79.4%**) have another verb one edit away ([QAC data](https://github.com/mustafa0x/quran-morphology)). The most complete published Arabic seed list, TZUR's, has 67.3% of its words one edit from another word. English BIP-39 has 36.8%, French 0.2% and SLIP-39 0% ([TZUR arabic.txt](https://github.com/osem23/bip39-wordlists-tzur/blob/main/wordlists/tzur-original/arabic.txt); [english.txt](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039/english.txt); [french.txt](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039/french.txt)). Bucks can afford the relaxed rule because its checksum is far stronger than BIP-39's. A 15-bit checksum misses about 1 random error in 32,768, while BIP-39's 4-bit checksum misses 1 in 16 ([BIP-39](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039.mediawiki)). With a strong checksum, typo distance matters less for detecting mistakes. It still matters for recovery, because the user needs to work out which word was written wrongly.

### A smaller list costs two to four extra words, not security

The list-design notes assumed BIP-39's checksum rule (one checksum bit per 32 entropy bits) and so concluded that smaller lists also weaken the checksum. That rule does not bind Bucks. Bucks fills whole words with entropy plus checksum, so a smaller list simply needs more words. The table below is plain arithmetic from Bucks's layout. The last column estimates how many wrong "valid" phrases appear if the wallet tries every single-word substitution to repair one mistyped word: the number of possible substitutions divided by the checksum space.

| List size | Bits per word | 128-bit phrase | Checksum bits | 256-bit phrase | Checksum bits | False repairs per typo (256-bit) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8192 (today) | 13 | 11 words | 15 | 21 words | 17 | ≈1.3 |
| 4096 | 12 | 12 words | 16 | 23 words | 20 | ≈0.1 |
| **2048** | 11 | **13 words** | **15** | **25 words** | **19** | **≈0.1** |
| 1024 | 10 | 15 words | 22 | 28 words | 24 | ≈0.002 |

At 2048 with 13 and 25 words, the checksum is exactly as strong as today (15 bits) or stronger (19 bits instead of 17). Automatic repair of one wrong word also becomes practical. Today a single typo in a 21-word phrase gives about 1.3 false candidates, so the wallet cannot fix it alone; at 25 words from 2048 it gives about 0.1. BIP-39 itself lists the inability to help correct errors as one of its shortcomings ([BIP-39](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039.mediawiki)). The real price of 2048 is two extra words at 128 bits and four at 256 bits. If any phrases have already been issued with the current list, that list must stay readable under a version tag (see the rules below).

## Verbs alone stop below 1,500 entries

A verbs-only list cannot reach even 2048, so the verdict is no. The Quran has **1,473–1,479 verb lemmas from 940 roots** ([QAC data](https://github.com/mustafa0x/quran-morphology); [QuranMorph](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18148v1)). By derived pattern, these are Form I 670, II 171, III 63, IV 263, V 81, VI 36, VII 15, VIII 101, IX 2 and X 71 ([QAC data](https://github.com/mustafa0x/quran-morphology)). Derived forms are real separate meanings, such as عَلِمَ "know", عَلَّمَ "teach" and تَعَلَّمَ "learn" ([QAC verb forms](https://corpus.quran.com/documentation/verbforms.jsp)). Without diacritics, however, many of them merge. The 1,473 verbs fold into **1,415 distinct spellings**, in 58 groups that clash. علم stands for both "knew" and "taught", كذب for "lied" and "denied", نزل for "descended" and "sent down", امن for "was safe" and "believed", and اتي for "came" and "gave". On top of that, **270 verbs share their bare spelling with a noun**, as in علم "knew"/"knowledge" and ذهب "went"/"gold". **55 verbs are only two letters long** once folded ([QAC data](https://github.com/mustafa0x/quran-morphology)). About **529 of the 1,475 QAC verb entries (36%) occur only once** ([QAC verbs p. 19](https://corpus.quran.com/verbs.jsp?page=19)). Under the strict one-typo rule, only **724 verbs** survive ([QAC data](https://github.com/mustafa0x/quran-morphology)).

Translation shrinks the pool further. Different Arabic verbs often become one English verb: عَلِمَ and عَرَفَ are both "know", and many Form I/Form IV pairs share one gloss. Religious screening removes verbs of acts reserved for God, such as خلق "create", رزق "provide", أحيا/أمات "give life/death", غفر "forgive" and أنزل "send down revelation". It also removes worship verbs and the strongly Quranic imperative قل "say!". This review estimates, without a measurement, that a verbs-only Quranic list ends at roughly 1,000 entries with a uniqueness rule and around 700 or fewer with the strict typo rule. Verbs do bring one real benefit, according to the religious research. Divine names, prophets and angels are nouns, so a list of verbs in dictionary form reads as a list of ordinary actions ([MuftiSays](https://islamqa.org/hanafi/muftisays/10078/how-to-dispose-of-leaflets-and-papers-with-allah-s-name-and-quranic-ayats/)). The right decision is therefore to **welcome verbs as a large share of a lemma list but not to require them**.

For the verbs that are included, the traditional citation form, the third-person masculine singular past (كتب), is the best choice. The French BIP-39 list made the same kind of choice with "only infinitive verbs" ([BIP-39 wordlists](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039/bip-0039-wordlists.md)). Its weakness is that it is short and collides often. So each bare spelling may hold only one verb, and a spelling is dropped when its noun reading is more common. The verbal noun (تعليم "teaching" versus علم) separates the derived forms more clearly, but it is grammatically a noun, many verbal nouns never occur in the Quran, and many start with إ or end in ة, which are both folding hazards. The present tense (يعلم) gains nothing, because Form I and Form II still collide. The imperative is the worst option: weak verbs shrink to one or two letters (قِ, كُلْ), and the words read as commands to the user.

## Arabic and translation lists need explicit, testable rules

BIP-39 itself sets only soft rules. The first four letters should identify a word, similar words should be avoided, and the list should be sorted ([BIP-39](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039.mediawiki)). The individual language lists added stricter rules of their own. They use one dictionary form per word (French: "only infinitive verbs"), allow no distinctions that depend on accents (Spanish, Czech, Portuguese), ban pairs that differ by one letter, ban homophones, and avoid offensive or negative words ([BIP-39 wordlists](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039/bip-0039-wordlists.md)). SLIP-39 is the strictest standard: 1024 words, unique four-letter prefixes and no two words within one edit. It also refuses translation completely ([SLIP-0039](https://github.com/satoshilabs/slips/blob/master/slip-0039.md)). Since 2020, BIP-39 has strongly discouraged non-English lists ([BIP-39](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039.mediawiki)), and an Arabic proposal in 2025 was closed within two days. Reviewers noted that its words were stored reversed and that it was unclear which variety of Arabic it used ([PR #1925](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/pull/1925)). Those objections concern BIP-39's text-based seed, which Bucks does not use. Monero already turns one secret into words in any language through list index numbers ([electrum-words.cpp](https://github.com/monero-project/monero/blob/master/src/mnemonics/electrum-words.cpp)). The closest prior art to Bucks's meaning-aligned design is the open 2026 TZUR draft. It ships index-paired Arabic, Urdu and Farsi display lists with a fixed list identifier and requires rejection of ambiguous input ([PR #2200](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/pull/2200)). Its Arabic list, however, stores 11 words twice, once with hamza and once without (إعلان/اعلان). It also lists one verb in two persons (تقدم/يقدم) ([TZUR arabic.txt](https://github.com/osem23/bip39-wordlists-tzur/blob/main/wordlists/tzur-original/arabic.txt)). Bucks should copy the draft's discipline and avoid its mistakes.

### Unicode normalisation does not fold Arabic, so Bucks needs its own fold

Unicode normalisation (NFC, NFKC, NFKD) never treats ى as ي, ة as ه, ٱ as ا, or the Persian and Urdu letters ی ک ہ as their Arabic equivalents. Tatweel and diacritics also survive every normalisation form ([Unicode 16.0, ch. 9](https://www.unicode.org/versions/Unicode16.0.0/core-spec/chapter-9/); [UnicodeData](https://www.unicode.org/Public/16.0.0/ucd/UnicodeData.txt)). Arabic search engines solve this with an explicit folding step, as in Lucene's Arabic normaliser and CAMeL Tools ([Lucene](https://lucene.apache.org/core/4_2_1/analyzers-common/org/apache/lucene/analysis/ar/ArabicNormalizer.html); [CAMeL Tools](https://camel-tools.readthedocs.io/en/latest/api/utils/normalize.html)). Bucks should define one fold function and use it both when building the list and when reading user input. The fold has seven steps. First, apply NFKC; avoid NFKD, because it splits أ into two characters. Second, delete all short-vowel marks, the dagger alef, Quranic marks, tatweel, zero-width joiners and direction-control characters. Third, change أ إ آ ٱ to ا. Fourth, change ى and ی to ي, ک to ك, and ة ہ ە to ه. Fifth, change ؤ to و and ئ to ي, and make one documented choice about standalone ء. Sixth, split input on any kind of space and trim it. Seventh, give the fold a version number. Without diacritics, Arabic is highly ambiguous: Modern Standard Arabic averages about 12 possible analyses per bare word ([Camelira](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.16807)). That is why rules A1 and A11 below are essential.

### Rules for the Arabic list

| Rule | What it means in plain words | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A1 Unique after folding | No two entries may look the same once diacritics and letter variants are removed | Stops duplicates like إعلان/اعلان and the 47% clashes in the current list |
| A2 Typo distance | No two entries one edit apart, at least among words of 4 letters or fewer; for all words if the count allows | One-edit neighbours are very common in Arabic |
| A3 Dot check | Avoid short entries that differ only by dots (يسر/بشر/نشر) | Handwritten backups lose or move dots; 938 Quranic lemmas share a dotless shape with another ([QAC data](https://github.com/mustafa0x/quran-morphology)) |
| A4 No attached words | No ال, no و ف ب ل ك س prefix, no pronoun ending (ه ها هم كم نا ي) | One entry per word: ربكم becomes رب |
| A5 One lexeme per entry | No two grammatical forms of the same lemma | TZUR's تقدم/يقدم mistake |
| A6 Allowed characters | The 28 letters plus ء أ إ آ ؤ ئ ة ى, stored in NFC without diacritics | No Persian letters, digits or hidden characters |
| A7 Typing order | Store words in logical order; test that the first stored letter is the first typed letter | The 2025 Arabic proposal stored words reversed ([PR #1925](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/pull/1925)) |
| A8 Prefix rule | Unique first 3 letters for words of 4+ letters; no entry is the start of another entry | "First 4 letters" is often the whole Arabic word; Monero sets prefix length per language ([Monero](https://github.com/monero-project/monero/tree/master/src/mnemonics)) |
| A9 Length | 3 to 7 letters after folding | One- and two-letter words break easily and look like particles |
| A10 Display | Show each word as its own right-to-left unit, with the number in a separate cell | Mixed-direction text reorders itself ([UAX #9](https://www.unicode.org/reports/tr9/)) |
| A11 One meaning without diacritics | Drop an entry whose bare spelling has a common second meaning, or fix it to one meaning | علم can be "knew", "taught" or "knowledge" |

Bucks should also state the variety plainly, for example "Classical Arabic vocabulary attested in the Quran, written in standard modern spelling". This answers the "which Arabic?" objection raised against the 2025 proposal.

### Rules for every translation list

| Rule | What it means in plain words |
|---|---|
| T1 Same count, same meaning | Exactly as many entries as the Arabic list; entry *i* means the same as Arabic entry *i* |
| T2 Unique after the language's own folding | Lower-case and remove accents (Urdu and Persian use the Arabic fold) before checking for duplicates |
| T3 One token | No spaces, hyphens or apostrophes; "know", not "to know"; join words only as a last resort |
| T4 Short-word distance | No one-letter-difference pairs among short words; publish prefix statistics instead of promising them |
| T5 Same word only at the same number | A word may appear in two lists only at the same index |
| T6 Meanings stay distinct in every language | Choose meanings jointly across all languages; drop pairs that collapse in any one of them |
| T7 Language detection | Script first, then whole-list match, then checksum, then ask the user; never guess silently |
| T8 Versioning | Store language, list version, fold version and the list's SHA-256 with each backup |
| T9 Arabic-script sister lists | Check Urdu and Persian against Arabic after folding |
| T10 Display | Words isolated for display; reject phrases that mix scripts |

Rule T5 is the one that index alignment depends on. English and French BIP-39 share 100 words, all at different positions, so a phrase typed in the wrong language can decode to a different secret ([english.txt](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039/english.txt); [french.txt](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/blob/master/bip-0039/french.txt)). For Bucks, a shared word at the *same* index is harmless, because it encodes the same value. Urdu will often use the same word as the Arabic entry (قلم "pen" in both, for example), and that is allowed. Only a shared word at a *different* index is dangerous. Rule T6 is the hardest rule, and it grows with list size. With 2048 meanings it is manageable. With 8192 meanings it becomes the main obstacle, because each meaning would need its own single word in English, Urdu and every other language at once. BIP-39's Japanese list shows how whitespace handling alone can break a reference implementation ([PR #2237](https://github.com/bitcoin/bips/pull/2237)), so all of these rules belong in automated tests, not in documentation alone.

## Licences allow the Arabic data only with permission; meanings must be written fresh

Every machine-readable multilingual word-by-word data set the research found is **per occurrence**. That means one gloss for each of the roughly 77,000 word positions, which changes with context. None of them is per lemma ([QUL word translation tool](https://qul.tarteel.ai/word_translations/1401); [QAC FAQ](https://corpus.quran.com/faq.jsp)). The research found **no open, per-lemma, multilingual, index-aligned Quranic gloss list**. This works in Bucks's favour. If Bucks writes its own meaning list, the result is a new work that Bucks owns. That fits the "All rights reserved" code, and the alignment between languages is designed in from the start instead of inherited from someone else's choices.

The QAC terms contradict themselves. The GPL allows changes, but the added notice forbids them, and the FAQ limits shared data to non-commercial research ([QAC download](https://corpus.quran.com/download/); [QAC FAQ](https://corpus.quran.com/faq.jsp)). If the GPL governs, shipping GPL-derived data inside closed-source software could create GPL obligations. Whether it does depends on whether a word list counts as part of the program or as separate data. Single facts such as "this word has this root" are probably not protected by copyright in many countries. A systematic selection, however, could fall under the UK's database right, because the QAC was built at the University of Leeds. These points are unresolved and need a lawyer. The safe path has six steps. First, ask Kais Dukes or the QAC project in writing for commercial, closed-source use of a derived lemma, root and part-of-speech list, offering attribution and a link. Second, ask Birzeit's SinaLab about the QuranMorph data licence, as a second source or a replacement. Third, credit Tanzil with a link, and optionally confirm by email that taking individual words is acceptable. Fourth, treat QUL, the Quran Foundation API and QuranWBW as terminology checks only, never as copied content. Fifth, put the attribution notices in the wallet's About screen and in the header of the word-list file. Sixth, write the English meanings fresh with Penrice and Lane as references, and commission native-speaker translators, ideally with Islamic-studies background, for Urdu and the other languages. If QAC refuses, project linguists can assign lemmas directly from the Tanzil text with public-domain Penrice and Lane as guides, or Bucks can use QuranMorph if SinaLab confirms a commercial-compatible licence.

## Scholars separate dictionary words from the mushaf, with caveats

No fatwa anywhere addresses seed phrases or cryptographic word lists built from Quranic vocabulary. Everything below is reasoned by analogy from rulings on paper, coins, screens and products, and none of it is a ruling. The classical rules attach to text written or recited *as Quran*. The Shafi'i manual *ʿUmdat al-Sālik* requires ritual purity to touch Quran "written for the purpose of study". The same tradition allows carrying money and clothing that bear Quranic inscriptions, and allows saying Quranic phrases with the intention of dhikr rather than recitation ([Shafiifiqh via IslamQA.org](https://islamqa.org/shafii/shafiifiqh/30007/can-one-in-major-ritual-impurity-recite-the-quran-what-about-carry-or-touch-it/)). Egypt's Dar al-Ifta (2009) allowed verses outside the mushaf to be written in ordinary modern spelling, on the ground that such writing does not count as a mushaf ([Dar al-Ifta 12390](https://www.dar-alifta.org/ar/Fatwa/Details/12390)). No source treats an isolated ordinary Arabic word as "Quran" just because it occurs in the Quran. Scholars do differ at the edges. Dar al-Ifta (1973) called printing Quranic text on coins *makruh* (disliked) because of the loss of reverence and handling by people in a state of impurity ([Dar al-Ifta 16252](https://www.dar-alifta.org/ar/fatwa/details/16252)). Muftis are split on touching Quran text shown on phone screens ([Askimam](https://islamqa.org/hanafi/askimam/102917/witr-qadha-is-wudhu-necessary-for-touching-the-phone-while-the-qur-an-app-is-on/); [Darul Ifta Birmingham](https://daruliftabirmingham.co.uk/?p=20614)). The gold-dinar heritage that Bucks draws on cuts both ways. Umayyad dinars carried Quranic text ([Yale VRME](https://vrme.yale.edu/posts/2024-08-07-dinar-of-al-walid-i)), but later jurists disliked the practice for exactly these purity reasons ([Dar al-Ifta 16252](https://www.dar-alifta.org/ar/fatwa/details/16252)).

The firmest guidance concerns names. Fatwa bodies broadly agree that paper bearing God's names, angels' names, prophets' names or verses must not go in the rubbish. They direct it to be buried, placed in running water, burned or shredded, and several Hanafi muftis require erasing the divine name first ([Askimam 11704](https://islamqa.org/hanafi/askimam/11704/is-it-permissible-to-throw-away-papers-with-the-name-of-allah-on-them-or-not-what-is-the-proper-way-to-dispose-of/); [al-Kawthari](https://islamqa.org/hanafi/daruliftaa/8512/using-a-paper-shredder-to-dispose-of-material-with-quranic-verses-on-them/); [Dar al-Ifta 6306](https://www.dar-alifta.org/en/fatwa/details/6306/throwing-papers-containing-quranic-verses-and-the-beautiful-names-of-allah-in-tr); [IslamQA 158570](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/158570)). Scholars separate names used only for God, such as Allah, al-Raḥmān, al-Rabb and al-Khāliq, from names shared with people, such as karīm, raḥīm and ḥakīm, which may describe humans and things ([IslamQA 314410](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/314410); [Islamweb 88517](https://islamweb.net/en/fatwa/88517/using-allaahs-names)). The disposal fatwas, however, do not exempt shared names. One Hanafi mufti even treats "Assalamu alaykum" as containing a divine name ([Askimam 11704](https://islamqa.org/hanafi/askimam/11704/is-it-permissible-to-throw-away-papers-with-the-name-of-allah-on-them-or-not-what-is-the-proper-way-to-dispose-of/)). The owner's own example, ربكم/ربهم/ربه, shows how the technical and religious rules meet. The lemma rule merges the forms into رب, and the religious rule then removes رب altogether.

Two other rulings shape the design. Reversing or reordering the words of the Quran (*tankīs*) is forbidden, because it takes God's speech out of the form in which it was revealed ([IslamOnline](https://fiqh.islamonline.net/%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%AA%D9%86%D9%83%D9%8A%D8%B3-%D9%81%D9%8A-%D9%82%D8%B1%D8%A7%D8%A1%D8%A9-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%82%D8%B1%D8%A2%D9%86/)). A random list of dictionary words is not offered as Quran, so it is not tankīs in the technical sense. Random phrases could still accidentally reproduce a Quranic phrase or put punishment words next to sacred ones. Malaysia's Federal Territories gazetted a fatwa in 2021 that prohibits using Quranic verses to promote products, as a way to block "selling religion" for worldly gain. Perlis and other states have similar fatwas ([Skrine](https://www.skrine.com/insights/alerts/july-2021/federal-territories-fatwa-sale-of-products-using-v); [Mufti Perlis](https://muftiperlis.gov.my/index.php/fatwa/49-muamalah/1389-ruling-on-the-sale-of-products-using-ruqyah-verses-verses-of-al-qur-an-for-commercial-purposes)). Single words are not verses, but marketing a wallet as "Quranic" for its religious appeal comes close to that concern. Existing Shariah reviews of gold and crypto tokens, such as Amanie Advisors' certification of HelloGold's GOLDX, covered asset backing and transaction certainty, not wording ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/amp/story/your-money%2Fcryptocurrency%2Fmalaysian-firm-adds-islamic-certification-to-cryptocurrency-1.2176424)). Bucks must therefore name the word list explicitly in any review it commissions.

Only the first two rows rest directly on cited rulings, and even there the shared names (karīm, raḥīm) are left out as a precaution, not because a ruling requires it. The other six rows are design precautions. They make sense because the cost is small, probably a few hundred lemmas, while the risk they remove is the one that would damage the project's reputation most. Three more measures complete the approach. First, call the list something like "Bucks Classical Arabic word list (every word appears in the Quran)" rather than "Quranic words". Second, give users one short, calm paragraph of guidance: these are ordinary Arabic words, not verses; keep backups off the floor and out of the rubbish; when retiring a paper backup, shred it and then bury or burn the remains, and grind or melt a metal one. Security and respect point the same way here. Third, note in that paragraph that Ibn Baz held that the writer bears no sin if someone else later discards such paper ([IslamQA 39376](https://islamqa.info/ar/ref/39376)). Index alignment also gives devout users a simple option: because every language encodes the same value, they can store the phrase in the English or Urdu list. The disposal rulings concern the Arabic text and, in one Hanafi answer, translations of the Quran itself, not dictionary meanings of single words ([Askimam 11704](https://islamqa.org/hanafi/askimam/11704/is-it-permissible-to-throw-away-papers-with-the-name-of-allah-on-them-or-not-what-is-the-proper-way-to-dispose-of/)).

## Recommended approach: a 2048-lemma list built and reviewed in twelve steps

**Selection level:** lemmas, meaning one entry per noun or adjective headword and one per verb (root plus pattern), drawn from all parts of speech but limited to content words. Prefer concrete nouns and everyday verbs, and prefer words that occur at least twice. **List size:** 2048, used as 13 words for 128-bit and 25 words for 256-bit secrets, which keeps 15-bit and 19-bit checksums. **Verbs only:** rejected, because the ceiling is below 1,500 before any filtering. Verbs remain welcome as a large share. **Spelling:** standard modern spelling in dictionary form, typed and matched without diacritics, with optional vowels for display only. This is safer for typing and, by the 2009 Egyptian ruling, does not read as mushaf text. **Distance rule:** unique after folding for all entries, no one-edit pairs among words of four letters or fewer, and the dot check on short words. A full one-edit ban is a goal to measure, not a promise. **Exclusions:** the eight categories above, plus particles, pronouns, proper names, words of one or two letters, and words whose bare spelling has two common meanings.

If the owner insists on 8192, the honest options are limited. A unique-meaning list of 8192 words that all appear in the Quran is impossible, because the Quran does not contain that many lemmas. Only one route reaches 8192 with unique meanings. It widens the source to Classical Arabic words built on Quranic roots and treats "appears in the Quran" as a preference rather than a rule; the list-design research recommends this route ([QAC data](https://github.com/mustafa0x/quran-morphology)). Its costs are real. It gives up the claim that every word is Quranic, roughly quadruples the translation and review work, and makes rule T6 (8192 meanings distinct in every language at once) the main obstacle. Nobody has yet counted whether a classical lexicon yields 8192 entries that pass the typo rules. The licence-clean sources for that wider pool are the public-domain Lane and Penrice texts, worked on by project linguists. Some tricks look like shortcuts but bring back the original problem, such as filling 8192 slots with four grammatical forms of each of 2048 lemmas: they recreate ربكم/ربهم/ربه and the typing clashes. Keeping the current word forms contradicts the owner's own goal.

| Step | Action | Pass condition or output |
|---|---|---|

## Conclusion

The owner's two goals, "one meaning per word" and "8192 words from the Quran", cannot both be met. The real decision is which one to keep. Keeping the Quranic source and moving to 2048 costs two or four extra words per phrase. In return the checksum is preserved or strengthened, one-word repair becomes practical, translation and scholarly review shrink to a quarter of the work, and the list becomes something Bucks can describe honestly as "every word appears in the Quran". Keeping 8192 means accepting that many words will not be Quranic, and taking on an untested translation problem four times larger. The decision should be made only after step 6 measures the true number of usable lemmas, because the 2,300–2,900 figure is still an estimate.

The work also showed that the technical, legal and religious constraints mostly push the same way. Dictionary forms in modern spelling are easier to type and less like scripture. Excluding names and ritual terms removes words that are both sensitive and prone to typing clashes. Writing the meaning lists in-house avoids every restrictive licence and builds the index alignment in by design. The current word-form list fails on all three counts at once. Its 47% typing clashes and its 61 spellings of رب are reason enough to replace it, whichever size the owner chooses.
